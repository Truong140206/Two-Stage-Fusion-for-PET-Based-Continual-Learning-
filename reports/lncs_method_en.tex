\begin{abstract}
Parameter-efficient continual learners maintain task-specific adapters whose
selection affects both routing and classification. We augment HRM-PET with a
random-projection (RP) head fitted by ridge regression over fixed first-task
LoRA features. Its sufficient statistics are accumulated
task by task without retaining examples. The same RP scores support two
inference-time operations: task-level fusion with the initial adapted prediction
before re-matching, and class-level fusion with the final logits using a
top-two-margin gate. Across three benchmarks and four seeds, DRM-proposal
accuracy increases by $1.10$--$2.26$ points and classification accuracy by
$0.69$--$1.50$, with no consistent improvement in forgetting or backward
transfer. Single-seed ablations across five backbones show that the routing
contribution depends on the gate. A four-seed comparison against ungated
class-only fusion favours the full method on ImageNet-R and CIFAR-100, but
does not resolve a difference on CUB-200; this comparison measures the joint
addition of routing and gating. A grid over the two mixing weights shows that
classification accuracy is nearly flat in the routing weight, so the reported
value is not a resolved optimum.

\keywords{Continual learning \and Parameter-efficient tuning \and Parameter
routing \and Random projection \and Signal fusion.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Continual learning asks a model to absorb a stream of tasks without losing what
earlier ones taught it. The dominant recent answer freezes a large pre-trained
backbone and attaches a small set of task-specific parameters --- prompts,
adapters, low-rank updates --- so that a new task writes into fresh capacity
instead of overwriting old weights. Forgetting stays low by construction and no
exemplars need be stored, which is why parameter-efficient tuning now leads the
exemplar-free setting~\cite{ref_l2p,ref_dualprompt,ref_hide}.

The design moves the difficulty rather than removing it. At inference the task
identity is unknown, so before the model can classify it must choose which
parameter set to attach. That choice is itself a prediction, and every later
computation inherits it: attach the wrong adapter and the classifier is asked to
work from features it was never adapted for. Successive methods have improved
the choice --- larger pools, attention-weighted mixtures, explicit re-matching
stages that revisit an initial guess --- but the choice still rests on evidence
drawn from one place, the backbone's own predictions.

We ask what a second, differently derived source of evidence would be worth, and
find that one is already available for the price of a forward pass. The features
such a learner computes anyway can be pushed through a frozen random projection
and read by a ridge classifier fitted in closed form from accumulated
second-order statistics, in the manner of RanPAC~\cite{ref_ranpac}. This head
stores no examples and changes no training procedure, and --- the property that
matters here --- it makes different mistakes from the routing module.

We reuse that one head at the two points where the pipeline commits: to revise
the task proposal before re-matching, and to blend with the final class logits
under a per-sample gate that opens only where the primary classifier is
undecided. Taking HRM-PET~\cite{ref_hrmpet} as the pipeline to build on, we
measure what each operation contributes, and say so where the evidence does not
settle the question.

\subsubsection{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We examine unused complementary evidence in
      HRM-PET~\cite{ref_hrmpet} and show that a better task proposal does not
      automatically become a better class prediction because the
      shared head frequently predicts the correct class in spite of a wrong
      route (Sect.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item We show that a second-order head over a frozen random projection of the
      existing features, fitted in closed form and never by gradient descent,
      supplies a complementary source of evidence with partially different
      errors from the first, and we fuse the two at the routing and the
      classification level alike, entirely at inference time.
\item We evaluate on three datasets over four seeds and report by axis: routing
      improves by $+1.10$ to $+2.26$ points and classification by $+0.69$ to
      $+1.50$, each several times the seed-to-seed standard deviation, while the
      two retention metrics show no consistent improvement
      (Sect.~\ref{sec:exp}).
\item We reconstruct the full grid the original work covers --- four datasets by
      five pre-training configurations, identified from traces in its released
      code. All $20$ cells improve on both accuracy axes, over baselines that
      span from $31.8$ to $93.8$ Acc@1 (Sect.~\ref{sec:grid}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsubsection{Parameter pools and the selection they require.}
L2P~\cite{ref_l2p} and DualPrompt~\cite{ref_dualprompt} set the shape most
parameter-efficient continual learners still follow: freeze the backbone, keep a
pool of lightweight parameter sets, and pick from it at inference using a query
from the bare backbone. CODA-Prompt~\cite{ref_coda} replaces the discrete pick
with an attention-weighted combination; HiDe-Prompt~\cite{ref_hide} separates
within-task prediction, task-identity inference and task-adaptive prediction and
optimises them apart, observing that identity inference, not within-task
classification, is what limits the pipeline. HRM-PET~\cite{ref_hrmpet}, our
baseline, substitutes LoRA adapters~\cite{ref_lora} for prompts and adds
re-matching stages that revisit the initial decision. Later work enlarges the
expert family: MoTE~\cite{ref_mote} combines task-specific experts, and
DLEPEM~\cite{ref_dlepem} combines general and adapted prototypes for matching.
Our contribution is specifically the reuse of a ridge head at both re-matching
and class prediction, rather than a claim that prior work never combines evidence.

\subsubsection{Closed-form solutions in continual learning.}
A separate line replaces gradient descent on the classifier with a solution
written in closed form, which removes the mechanism by which sequential updates
overwrite one another. Nearest-class-mean prototypes are the simplest instance,
and ADaM~\cite{ref_adam} adapts the backbone on the first task only, then reads
frozen features. Analytic continual learning states the problem as least squares
directly: ACIL~\cite{ref_acil} recasts class-incremental learning as a recursive
least-squares problem in which a correlation matrix rather than a sample buffer
carries the past, yielding a model equal to the one a joint fit would produce,
and DS-AL~\cite{ref_dsal} adds a second stream to recover nonlinear capacity
that a single linear solve gives up. RanPAC~\cite{ref_ranpac} reaches the same
form from the feature side: frozen features through a fixed random projection,
second-order statistics accumulated across tasks, one ridge system solved. On
Split-ImageNet-R it reports a nonlinear projection head that outperforms its
joint linear probe~\cite{ref_ranpac}.

Our head belongs to this family and we claim no novelty in it --- the
projection, the Gram accumulation and the ridge solve are RanPAC's. What differs
is its role. These methods use the closed-form classifier as the entire
predictor and discard routing; we keep the routing pipeline and consult the same
solved head twice, once on the task decision and once on the class decision.

\subsubsection{Correcting the classifier after the fact.}
Class-incremental classifiers lean towards recent classes, and the survey of
Masana et al.~\cite{ref_survey} locates the bias in both the bias terms and the
classifier weight norms. Corrections follow the diagnosis:
iCaRL~\cite{ref_icarl} classifies to the nearest exemplar mean;
BiC~\cite{ref_bic} learns a per-task affine map on the logits from a held-out
split; Weight Aligning~\cite{ref_wa} rescales new-class weight norms to match
the old; LUCIR~\cite{ref_lucir} normalises features and weights before the
softmax. We keep the adapters, auxiliary classifier and shared head fixed,
and retain no exemplars. Current-task training data are still needed to fit
the RP head. The fusion of Sect.~\ref{sec:method} acts on scores at inference;
its extra fixed-adapter pass is included in the reported cost.

\subsection{HRM-PET: The Baseline We Build On}

We use the released HRM-PET~\cite{ref_hrmpet} inference path. The diagnostics
quoted in this subsection are measurements at seed $42$; they motivate the
design and are separate from the evaluation of Sect.~\ref{sec:exp}.

\subsubsection{Task-identity inference (TII).}
The input image is passed through the \emph{bare} backbone, with no adapter
attached, to obtain a query feature. That feature enters an auxiliary classifier
$g_\omega$ trained with cross-entropy, producing unnormalised class logits:
\begin{equation}
q(x) = h(x;\theta_{\mathrm{ptm}}),
\qquad
u = g\!\left(q(x); \theta_\omega\right).
\label{eq:tii}
\end{equation}
The initial match is $t_0=\mathcal T(\arg\max_c u_c)$.

\subsubsection{Re-matching (DRM and CRM).}
The first adapted pass attaches $p_{t_0}$ and produces
$r=g_{\theta_g}(h(x;p_{t_0},\theta_{\rm ptm}))$. DRM proposes the task of
$\arg\max r$ and evaluates its adapter when it differs from $t_0$.
The proposed fusion modifies this task proposal, not the preceding bare TII
pass. CRM processes low-confidence samples selected from $r$ using the
baseline's generalised-entropy threshold. It compares the current logits with
an alternative adapter associated with the second-ranked class of $r$, using
the baseline log-sum-exp score. Thus CRM is confidence-triggered; it is not
restricted to cases where the two task identities agree. Both operations reuse
trained modules and can require further adapter forwards. Re-matching is a
heuristic, not a guarantee that classification accuracy cannot decrease.

\subsubsection{Shared classification head.}
The selected parameter set is attached to the backbone, and the shared head
produces a score vector over all classes seen so far:
\begin{equation}
\ell = g\!\left(h(x; p_{\hat{t}}, \theta_{\mathrm{ptm}}); \theta_g\right)
   \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|},
\qquad
\hat{y} = \arg\max_c\, \ell_c ,
\label{eq:logits}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{t}$ is the identity after DRM and CRM. Classes not yet seen are
masked with $-\infty$.

\subsubsection{First weakness: unused complementary evidence.}
The baseline derives its routing evidence from TII and subsequent shared-head
predictions, without an independently fitted RP head. To see what a second
source would be worth we built the head of Sect.~\ref{sec:rp} on HRM-PET's own
LoRA features and used it as a router. At seed 42 on ImageNet-R, TII routes
$63.80\%$ of samples correctly, HRM-PET's DRM-proposal metric is $77.79\%$ and
the RP head alone $77.12\%$, while the \emph{union} of the two --- the fraction
at least one routes correctly --- reaches $82.99\%$, against $92.86\%$ on
CIFAR-100 and $96.26\%$ on CUB-200.

They therefore fail on \emph{different} samples: the union exceeds TII by
$19.19$ points of the ImageNet-R test set, and since TII is wrong on $36.20\%$
of it, those points are $53.0\%$ of TII's errors repaired by the head, against
$62.0\%$ and $46.9\%$ on the other two. Had they failed together the union
could not exceed the stronger of them. The union is an oracle: it uses the
labels to ask what a perfect combiner could reach, and no router we build
attains it. This diagnostic compares bare TII with RP, not $r$ with $s$, the
actual Stage 1 inputs.

\subsubsection{Second weakness: better routing is not better classification.}
$\ell$ in (\ref{eq:logits}) comes from the \emph{shared} head, which spans every
class seen so far, so a mis-routed sample is frequently classified correctly
anyway: correcting its route buys nothing, and re-routing a sample that was
already correct can break it. Aggregate accuracy differences therefore do not
measure the fraction of routing repairs that also repair a class prediction.

The RP head is also a complete classifier, and a routing-only variant discards
that evidence. On ImageNet-R the routed head classifies $74.31\%$ correctly and
the projection head $70.08\%$, yet their union reaches $78.89\%$: even four
points weaker overall, the head alone contributes $4.58$ percentage points of
the test set to this union, and $2.43$ and $3.87$ on CIFAR-100 and CUB-200.
These two measurements give the two components below.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Class-incremental learning (CIL) learns a sequence of $T$ tasks without
forgetting earlier ones. The $t$-th task consists of $n_t$ samples
$\mathcal{D}_t=\{(x^t_i,y^t_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_t}$ drawn from a class set
$\mathcal{C}_t$, where $x^t_i \in \mathcal{X}$ is an input image and
$y^t_i \in \mathcal{Y}$ its label.

Three constraints define the setting. At step $t$ only $\mathcal{D}_t$ is
available for training. The class sets are disjoint,
$\mathcal{C}_t \cap \mathcal{C}_{t'} = \emptyset$ for all $t \neq t'$, and at
inference the \emph{task identity is unknown}: the model must discriminate among
all classes seen so far through a single shared classification head. Finally,
this work operates in the exemplar-free setting: images from past tasks are
never retained.

In methods based on parameter-efficient tuning
(PET)~\cite{ref_l2p,ref_dualprompt,ref_hide}, a pre-trained backbone
$h_{\mathrm{ptm}}$ with parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{ptm}}$ is frozen and a
lightweight parameter pool $\mathcal{P}=\{p_1,\dots,p_T\}$ is maintained, each
set trained on one task; here every $p_t$ is a LoRA adapter~\cite{ref_lora}. The
shared classification head is denoted $g_{\theta_g}$. Because the task identity
is unknown at inference, the system is forced to \emph{choose} which parameter
set to attach to the backbone before classifying, and everything downstream
depends on that choice.

We distinguish four class-score vectors: bare TII scores $u$, initial adapted
shared-head logits $r$, final re-matched logits $\ell$, and RP scores $s$.
The class-to-task map is $\mathcal T(\cdot)$; $t_0$ is the initial TII match and
$\hat t_{\rm DRM}$ the task proposed for direct re-matching. We write
$z(\cdot)$ for per-sample standardisation with the population standard
deviation floored at $10^{-6}$.

\subsection{The Second Source of Evidence}
\label{sec:rp}

We fit an RP head after each task and reuse its scores in two inference-time
fusion operations. HRM-PET parameters are not retrained.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} distinguishes the initial adapted logits from the
bare TII scores and shows both reuse paths.

The RP head follows RanPAC~\cite{ref_ranpac}. Features
$f\in\mathbb R^d$, $d=768$, are column vectors and come from ViT-B/16 with the
first task's LoRA adapter ($p_1$ here, adapter index $0$ in code), fixed during
accumulation and inference. No feature normalisation is applied. Changing
adapters between tasks would mix incompatible feature spaces.

\subsubsection{Projection and second-order statistics.}
The feature is projected into a high-dimensional space of size $M = 10^4$ and
passed through a nonlinearity, and the projected features are accumulated into
two matrices:
\begin{align}
h &= \mathrm{ReLU}(W^\top f) \in \mathbb{R}^{M},
\qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times M},\quad W_{jk}\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\label{eq:proj}\\
G &= \sum_n h_n h_n^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times M},
\qquad
C = \sum_n h_n\, \mathbf{e}_{y_n}^{\top}
   \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times |\mathcal{Y}|},
\label{eq:gram}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{e}_{y}$ is the basis vector of class $y$. Each current-task
training sample contributes once; $G$ and $C$ are accumulated in float64.
$W$ is generated once with seed $1993$ and then frozen.

The nonlinear expansion permits richer decision functions than a linear head
on $f$. Without ReLU, $W^\top f$ remains linear in $f$ and adds no separating
power. This does not guarantee that the expanded data become linearly separable.

\subsubsection{Classification by ridge regression.}
The output weights are the closed-form solution of a regularised least-squares
problem:
\begin{equation}
W_{\mathrm{out}} = (G + \lambda I)^{-1} C \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times |\mathcal{Y}|},
\qquad
s = W_{\mathrm{out}}^{\top} h \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|} .
\label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}
The implementation solves the regularised system rather than explicitly
inverting it. The map takes $768$ input features through $10^4$ nonlinear
features to $|\mathcal Y|$ class scores.

\subsubsection{Two inherited properties.}
Both $G$ and $C$ in (\ref{eq:gram}) are \emph{sums} over samples, and addition
commutes, so for a \emph{fixed} feature map the accumulated statistics, and with
them $W_{\mathrm{out}}$, do not depend on the order in which the samples
arrived. Nothing an earlier task contributed is overwritten or decayed: the head
after $T$ tasks is the ridge solution of a joint fit over every feature seen so
far, and it reaches that solution having visited each task once.

This is weaker than an absence of forgetting and we claim no more than it says.
Adding a task adds classes that compete for the same arg max, so accuracy on old
classes can fall while their statistics sit untouched. Nor is the feature map
fixed across orderings: features come from the first task's adapter, so a
different task order yields a different extractor and a different $G$. The
property rules out one mechanism of forgetting --- the overwriting of old
evidence --- not the phenomenon. At the same time the sizes of the two matrices
do not depend on how many samples have been seen, and no per-sample feature is
ever retained, so the exemplar-free protocol is preserved by the structure of
the computation itself.

\subsection{Two-Stage Fusion}

Both stages blend two vectors that live on different scales: the ridge scores $s$
span roughly one unit while the logits $\ell$ span roughly thirty-five, so a
direct blend would let $\ell$ dominate at every weight. Each source is therefore
standardised per sample before blending:
\begin{equation}
z(v) = \frac{v - \mathrm{mean}(v)}{\sigma_\epsilon(v)},
\qquad \sigma_\epsilon(v)=\max\{\mathrm{std}(v),10^{-6}\},
\label{eq:znorm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{std}$ is the population standard deviation. Moments are
over seen tasks in Stage 1 and seen classes in Stage 2, excluding unseen entries.

\subsubsection{First stage: fusion at the routing level.}
Stage 1 runs after the initial adapted pass and before DRM. Its input is $r$,
the shared-head logits under the adapter selected by bare TII, not auxiliary
logits $u$. Both $r$ and RP scores $s$ are mapped to seen tasks:
\begin{equation}
r^{\mathrm{task}}_t = \max_{c \in \mathcal{C}_t} r_c ,
\qquad
s^{\mathrm{task}}_t = \max_{c \in \mathcal{C}_t} s_c ,
\qquad t = 1,\dots,\hat{T},
\label{eq:taskscore}
\end{equation}
with $\hat{T}$ the number of tasks seen so far; classes of unseen tasks never
enter either maximum. The two vectors are blended after standardisation:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{fused} = w\, z(r^{\mathrm{task}}) + (1-w)\, z(s^{\mathrm{task}}),
\qquad
\hat{t}_{\rm DRM} = \arg\max_t\, \mathrm{fused}_t ,
\qquad w = 0.7 .
\label{eq:route}
\end{equation}
For one seen task the standard deviation is zero, so the floor keeps the
operation finite. The fused task replaces the baseline DRM proposal
$\mathcal T(\arg\max r)$; the initial TII match, CRM candidate rule, confidence
test and shared head are unchanged. At $w=1$, the ordering is that of
$r^{\rm task}$ and the baseline DRM proposal is recovered. Recovering the
full baseline additionally requires $\beta=0$.

\subsubsection{Second stage: gated fusion at the classification level.}
Because $\ell$ comes from the shared head, it frequently predicts the correct
class in spite of a wrong routing decision. We therefore use the
random-projection head itself as a secondary classifier, this time taking its
class scores $s$.

A fixed blend can alter a confident primary prediction. We instead reduce
the RP share as the top-two probability margin grows, recognising that
confident predictions can still be wrong:
\begin{equation}
\beta_i = \beta \left( 1 - \frac{p_{(1)} - p_{(2)}}{p_{(1)} + p_{(2)}} \right),
\qquad \beta = 0.5 ,
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $p_{(1)} \geq p_{(2)}$ are the two largest probabilities of
$\mathrm{softmax}(\ell)$ over the classes seen so far. Dividing by the sum makes
the quantity depend only on the ratio $p_{(2)}/p_{(1)}$, that is, it measures how
threatened the top class is, independently of how much probability mass the pair
holds. The RP share approaches zero as the top class dominates and reaches
$\beta$ when the top two probabilities are equal; this is a continuous weight,
not a binary confidence test.

The blend reuses (\ref{eq:znorm}) and then maps the result back onto the scale of
$\ell$:
\begin{align}
m_i &= (1 - \beta_i)\, z(\ell_i) + \beta_i\, z(s_i),
\label{eq:mix}\\
\hat{\ell}_i &= m_i \cdot \sigma_\epsilon(\ell_i) + \mathrm{mean}(\ell_i)
   \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}.
\label{eq:affine}
\end{align}
The positive affine map-back preserves rankings and restores a reference
scale for cross-entropy, not a calibration guarantee. For standardised
nondegenerate vectors with correlation $\rho$, when the floor is inactive,
the mixture variance is
$(1-\beta_i)^2+\beta_i^2+2\beta_i(1-\beta_i)\rho$, which need not equal one.
At $\beta=0$, this stage leaves $\ell$ unchanged.

\subsubsection{Cost.}
At inference the head costs one extra adapter forward per sample, and the same
result serves both stages. That is the head's own cost, not the pipeline's
difference: changing the DRM proposal can change the number of additional
adapter evaluations. We did not separately measure the pipeline cost difference
relative to the baseline.

Per task the head accumulates $G$ and $C$ over the current task's training split
and solves one ridge system, so its footprint is set by $M$, not by how many
samples have been seen --- and it is not small. At $M = 10^4$ the Gram matrix
holds $10^8$ entries, $800$~MB in the float64 the solve requires, and the
Cholesky factor with the regularised copy brings the peak near $2.4$~GB. A
complete ten-task evaluation on ImageNet-R takes $283$~s on one RTX 4090 at
batch $24$; we report the total because the three phases were not instrumented
separately.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The backbone is a ViT-B/16 pre-trained on ImageNet-21K and adapted with LoRA of
rank $8$. The four-seed study uses Split-ImageNet-R, Split-CIFAR-100 and
Split-CUB-200 in $10$ tasks each; the grid of Sect.~\ref{sec:grid} replaces
CUB-200 with Split-ImageNet-A and 5-Datasets, the latter a $5$-task sequence.
The five pre-training settings are Sup-21K, MoCo-v3, iBOT-1K, iBOT-21K and DINO.
Seeds are $42$ to $45$, with the LoRA pool and the TII module retrained from
scratch for each. Baseline and proposed method are evaluated from the same
checkpoints through the same code path, avoiding differences due to separate
training runs within each seed. The projection head uses $M = 10^4$ dimensions,
a ReLU nonlinearity and $\lambda = 10^4$; no temperature scaling is applied.
Fusion starts at task 1. We preserve the baseline CRM settings
(\texttt{En=gen}, threshold $-10$, and two class-derived candidate tasks).

Acc@task in the released evaluator measures the DRM task proposal. In the
default class-based routing path, CRM can change the output logits without
updating the task identity passed to this metric; it is therefore not a measure
of final adapter selection after CRM. Acc@1, Acc@5 and Loss use the final
classification logits. Writing $a_{i,t}$ for classification accuracy on task $i$
at stage $t$, the retention measures are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Forgetting}
 &= \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}
 \left(\max_{i\le t\le T} a_{i,t}-a_{i,T}\right),\\
\mathrm{Backward}
 &= \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}(a_{i,T}-a_{i,i}).
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
Both averages exclude the last task. The maximum includes only stages at which
task $i$ has been learned. Lower is better for Loss and Forgetting; higher
is better for the other metrics.

\subsubsection{Sanity check.}
$w = 1.0$ in (\ref{eq:route}) restores the original DRM proposal and
$\beta = 0$ in (\ref{eq:mix}) leaves $\hat{\ell} = \ell$ element-wise. Both are
needed, each governing one stage. With the pair set the method reproduces the
HRM-PET baseline digit for digit on all three datasets --- $77.7914$,
$74.0191$, $1.2305$, the rest agreeing to the fourth decimal --- so every
difference in Table~\ref{tab:main} comes from the added components and not from
the evaluation path. Each of $w$ and $\beta$ doubles as a continuous ablation
axis for its own stage.

\subsection{The Full Grid}
\label{sec:grid}

Everything above rests on one backbone. The original work covers four datasets
by five pre-training configurations --- twenty cells, identified from traces in
its released code --- and we rebuilt every one, evaluating both methods through
one code path at seed $42$. \emph{Every cell} improves on both accuracy axes:
Acc@task by $+0.97$ to $+4.57$ and Acc@1 by $+0.59$ to $+3.58$, against
baselines spanning $31.8$ to $93.8$ Acc@1. Read as relative error reduction the
effect is largest where the baseline is strongest, up to $27.8\%$ on
5-Datasets.

Retention over those twenty cells behaves in a way the summary metric hides.
Backward averages $a_{i,T} - a_{i,i}$, final accuracy minus the accuracy each
task reached when it was learned. It therefore depends on both quantities.
Both halves rise here: final accuracy on earlier tasks improves in \emph{every
one} of the twenty cells, by $+1.83$ on average, while accuracy at learning time
improves by $+1.58$. Backward is the small residue of two larger movements, and
in the five cells where Backward worsens, the gain at learning time exceeds
the gain in final old-task accuracy. This explains the change but does not
establish an absence of forgetting.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

We ablate \emph{cumulatively}, as the original work does, on ImageNet-R at seed
$42$ across its five pre-training configurations, whose baselines run from
$68.47$ to $74.02$ Acc@1. Differences are reported below rather than absolute
values, since only the differences carry the argument.

\subsubsection{Routing gains depend on the gate.}
Each stage alone beats the baseline on every backbone --- routing by $+0.29$ to
$+0.74$, the classifier by $+0.51$ to $+3.07$ --- and they are far from additive.
With the gate off, adding routing to class fusion moves Acc@1 by only
$-0.07$ to $+0.02$. This near-zero net effect does not establish identical
predictions or repairs: improvements and newly introduced errors may cancel.
A prediction-level transition audit would be needed to distinguish these cases.

With the gate on, routing adds $+0.13$ to $+0.77$ and is positive on all five
backbones. The gate itself changes Acc@1 by $-1.32$ to $+0.85$ with class-only
fusion, compared with $-0.54$ to $+0.96$ when routing is present. This shows an
interaction. Re-matching may change the confidence on which the gate acts, but
aggregate accuracies do not identify which predictions are repaired or harmed.

routing method reports in its own right. But the two Acc@1 contributions must
not be added. Note also that the classifier's isolated contribution is smallest
by far on Sup-21K, $+0.51$ against $+2.23$ to $+3.07$ on the other four --- and
Sup-21K is the backbone every design decision was taken on.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Mixing Weights}
\label{sec:weights}

The two weights were fixed sequentially: $w$ before the second stage existed,
$\beta$ afterwards. Because both act on the same prediction, this raises the
question of whether the second stage shifts the optimum of the first.
We measured Acc@1 over the joint grid
$w \in \{0.6, 0.7, 0.8\} \times \beta \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ on ImageNet-R at
seed $42$ with the margin gate active.

The two barely interact. $\beta = 0.5$ is best at all three values of $w$ ---
giving $75.61$, $75.51$ and $75.50$ for $w = 0.6$, $0.7$ and $0.8$ --- and its
neighbours lose by comparable amounts in every row, the whole grid spanning
$75.12$ to $75.61$. Tuning the two in sequence therefore costs nothing
measurable here.

Acc@1 is also nearly flat in $w$, while the routing axis is not. At
$\beta = 0.5$ the three values of $w$ span $0.11$ points of Acc@1, whereas
Acc@task spans $1.01$: $80.39$, $79.97$ and $79.37$. Lowering $w$ buys routing
accuracy of which roughly one ninth reaches Acc@1 --- the second weakness of
Sect.~\ref{sec:method}, measured along a continuous axis rather than asserted.

We therefore do not claim $w = 0.7$ is optimal. Over four seeds $w = 0.6$ leads
by $0.17$ points and on this grid by $0.10$, both smaller than the $0.416$
seed-to-seed standard deviation of the Acc@1 change itself, so the two are
unresolved at this sample size. We report $w = 0.7$ because every other number
in this paper was measured at it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Design choices were explored on ImageNet-R, so its results are not independent
of model selection. The research record also includes development experiments
on CUB-200; it is not an untouched holdout. CIFAR-100 tests transfer of the
reported configuration, but additional seeds do not remove selection bias.
The main comparison covers four seeds on one backbone; the broader grid, the
conditional routing ablation and the weight grid use one seed.
Table~\ref{tab:classonly} changes routing and gating jointly; a four-seed
class-plus-gate control is still needed to isolate routing. The rows quoted in
Table~\ref{tab:prior} come from another implementation, and our reproduction
differs from the published baseline by $-2.62$ points on 5-Datasets. We claim
no consistent retention benefit or non-inferiority. Runtime is not split into
accumulation, solving and inference; prediction-level repair/harm counts were
not measured.

\section{Conclusion}

A fixed-feature RP classifier complements HRM-PET at two decisions: the DRM
task proposal and final classification. The full method improves routing and
classification across three datasets and four seeds, with Acc@1 gains of
$0.69$--$1.50$ percentage points. A single-seed twenty-cell study extends this
evidence across pre-training settings. Ablations show a gate-dependent routing
contribution; the four-seed comparison supports the joint addition of routing
and gating on two of three datasets. A joint grid over the two mixing weights
shows they barely interact and that classification accuracy is nearly flat in
the routing weight, so the reported $w$ is not a resolved optimum. These
accuracy gains do not establish a consistent reduction in forgetting. The
method trades an additional fixed-adapter forward and task-wise ridge fitting
for evidence reused at both decisions.
